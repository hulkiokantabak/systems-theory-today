SEGMENT B05 (domain: urban-heat)

Neighborhood Tree Canopy and Summer Surface Temperature: A Two-Year Study

This study asks whether the canopy-expansion program lowered daytime surface temperatures in the target neighborhood. It reports that mean afternoon surface temperature in the planted blocks fell by 1.4 degrees relative to the unplanted comparison blocks over two summers, and concludes the program modestly cooled the planted blocks.

A quiet correction to the comparison is warranted. The planted and comparison blocks were matched on building height and street width, but not on surface material; the comparison blocks are disproportionately light-colored concrete, while the planted blocks are darker asphalt. Because the two sets started from different baseline temperatures, part of the 1.4-degree difference is a material difference, not a canopy effect. Re-estimating with the first-summer baseline differenced out reduces the cooling to 0.9 degrees, which the study now reports as its central figure; the direction is unchanged and the modest-cooling conclusion holds on the smaller number.

The measure itself deserves a plain note. The study records surface temperature from overhead imagery, which is what the instrument can see; it does not record air temperature at pedestrian height, and the two are not interchangeable. The cooling reported here is a surface-temperature result, and the study does not claim a pedestrian-comfort result it did not measure.

One cross-reference should be fixed. The abstract gives the planted-block count as forty; the site table and every calculation use forty-two. The analysis is consistent on forty-two throughout; the abstract number is a miscount and does not touch the estimate.

The study recommends continuing the program and pairing future monitoring with pedestrian-height sensors.
