**Round 1 — Steelmen.**

*Turchin (strongest YES):* The wealth pump's mechanism is precise: sustained transfer of surplus from wage-earners to asset-holders inflates exactly the goods that gate family formation — housing, credentialing, childcare. Children become the most expensive positional purchase a household makes. Where the pump runs hottest, fertility craters hardest: South Korea and urban China combine extreme educational arms races and housing-cost-to-income ratios with the lowest TFRs ever recorded. The sign is negative — D3 up, S1 down — and the mechanism is affordability of adult life-milestones, not preference change. Timing fits: the post-2008 "second decline" across the OECD tracks asset-price divergence, not any new gain in schooling.

*Meadows (strongest NO, drafted before any consensus):* The demographic transition is the best-attested regularity in social science, and its driver is development — female education, urbanization, opportunity cost, mortality decline. Fertility fell first and fastest in places and periods where the wealth pump was weak or reversing (postwar Europe, 1950s–70s). The Nordic case cuts hardest: low inequality, decommodified childcare, generous transfers — and TFR still fell from ~1.9 to ~1.4 after 2010. If D3 were the driver, the Nordics should be the exception; they are the rule. The D3→S1 correlation is confounded: development produces both education-driven fertility decline and, later, financialized asset inequality. Delete the arrow or demote it to dashed.

**Round 2 — Positions.** Turchin: negative arrow, load-bearing. Meadows: no arrow; development/education absorbs the variance. Ibn Khaldun sides with Turchin on mechanism (elite overproduction devalues the credentials households are saving to buy, extending the treadmill into the fertile years) but concedes the cross-national gradient belongs to development.

**Round 3 — Adversary against the emerging middle.** *Nietzsche, summoned:* You are converging on a tidy compromise — "amplifier, not cause" — and both camps assume fertility is a budget line. But the Nordic anomaly refutes the cost theory as cleanly as the education theory: costs were socialized and fertility still fell. The post-2010 decline is anomie-shaped (S4) and optimization-shaped (D2): the life-script that made children non-optional dissolved, and every remaining choice is run through a cost-benefit engine that children always lose. D3 may correlate only because D2 drives both the pump and the audit of parenthood. Your "surviving arrow" may be a shadow of a deeper edge, D2→S1.

**Chair synthesis (non-voting).** Agreement-strength: **majority**, not consensus. Majority position: the arrow D3→S1 exists with **negative sign**, but as a *second-phase amplifier* — it does not explain the classic demographic transition (that variance belongs to development/education, conceded to Meadows) but does explain residual, within-country, post-transition variation: the gap between stated fertility intentions and achieved fertility widens with housing and credential-cost inflation, and the post-2008 decline steepens where asset inequality grew fastest. So the arrow *partially* survives the confound: it fails cross-nationally in levels, survives in within-country changes and in the intentions–outcomes gap. Preserved dissents: Meadows maintains the residual evidence is too weak to warrant a solid edge (dashed at most); Nietzsche maintains the arrow is epiphenomenal to D2/S4, and notes the Nordic anomaly remains unexplained by *both* majority and Meadows. Recommendation for the model: keep D3→S1, negative, weight "moderate/conditional," annotated as post-transition amplifier; log the D2→S1 direct edge as an open rival.
