The honest answer is that the arrow runs both ways, and the interesting question is not which direction is real but which direction dominates at which phase of the cycle. The map already commits to this: Y3 → S3 as a first-order link (institutional thinning removes the frames that make institutions feel responsive, opening space for actors who promise to bypass them) and S3 → Y3 as a reaction arrow, together forming the reinforcing loop R1. Populism is best read as a symptom that becomes a driver — a downstream expression of decay that, once in office or merely in dominance of the discourse, degrades the very capacities whose failure produced it: purging professional staff, delegitimizing courts and statistics agencies, converting procedural norms into partisan weapons.

But "both directions" is a lazy resting place unless the two arrows can be separated empirically. Four discriminators would do real work.

First, temporal precedence at fine grain. If Y3 → S3 dominates, objective measures of institutional performance — permitting times, state capacity indices, trust surveys, responsiveness of legislatures to median-voter preferences — should deteriorate measurably *before* populist vote shares rise, with a lag of years. If S3 → Y3 dominates, decay indicators should accelerate *after* populist electoral breakthroughs, from an otherwise stable baseline. Cross-national panel data with staggered populist entry (Hungary 2010, Poland 2015, Brazil 2018, the US 2016) gives natural event-study structure: does the pre-trend in institutional quality slope downward before the breakthrough, or is the kink at the election?

Second, the counterfactual of near-miss cases. Countries where populist movements surged but never took power (France repeatedly, Netherlands) isolate the S3 → Y3 channel: if institutions there decay at the same rate as in countries where populists governed, the causal weight of populist *rule* is small and the shared upstream cause — Y3 or, per Turchin's rival account, D3's wealth pump driving both — carries the load. If near-miss countries' institutions hold up markedly better, S3 → Y3 is doing real damage.

Third, sectoral specificity. Y3 → S3 predicts that populist support concentrates where institutional failure is *experienced* — regions with collapsed local services, hollowed-out labor-market institutions, closed hospitals — regardless of national rhetoric. S3 → Y3 predicts decay concentrated in the institutions populists *target* — courts, media regulators, electoral bodies — while untargeted technical agencies persist. The two signatures are distinguishable in administrative data.

Fourth, the D3 confound must be handled, because the strongest rival to both directions is that neither drives the other directly: elite overproduction and immiseration (D3) drive Y3 and S3 in parallel, and the Y3 ⇄ S3 correlation is partly spurious. Conditioning on inequality and relative-wage trajectories before estimating either arrow is the minimum standard; if the Y3–S3 association survives, the loop is real rather than an artifact of a common cause.

The synthesis the evidence most plausibly supports: Y3 → S3 is the initiating arrow — populism does not arise in high-capacity, responsive polities — while S3 → Y3 is the amplifying arrow that converts a linear decline into the vicious cycle R1. This matters for Theory A, because a reinforcing loop is exactly what turns a fixed adaptation gap G into a widening one: the political response to institutional failure makes adaptation slower, which deepens the failure that fed the response. The test, then, is not "which direction," but whether the loop gain exceeds one — whether each turn of the cycle is larger than the last. Amplitude-growth of successive populist waves within a country is the observable that answers it.
