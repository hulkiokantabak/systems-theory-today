The claim is falsifiable in principle with existing instruments, but only in a weakened, operationalized form — and the honest answer requires separating three things the claim bundles together: measurement, temporal ordering, and causal independence.

Measurement is the least of the problems. Anomie and loneliness are among the better-instrumented constructs in social science: the UCLA Loneliness Scale, the De Jong Gierveld scale, Srole's anomia items, the World Values Survey and European Social Survey trust and belonging batteries, Gallup's social-connection modules, and the General Social Survey's decades-long series on confidants and membership. These instruments have known psychometrics, cross-national coverage, and — crucially — long panels in several countries (SOEP, HILDA, UKHLS, Add Health). So S4 can be measured, repeatedly, in the same individuals over time. That is the raw material falsification needs.

The downstream-symptom claim, restated testably, says: variance in S4 is explained by prior states of the drivers and dynamics (D1–D4, Y1–Y3), and residual S4 does not predict future movement in those upstream nodes once its own upstream causes are controlled. Two observable signatures follow. First, in panel data, lagged measures of the posited causes — attention-economy exposure (S2 overlaps here), economic precarity as a D3 proxy, institutional trust as a Y3 proxy — should Granger-predict individual loneliness trajectories, while lagged loneliness should not predict those upstream variables net of controls. Cross-lagged panel models and their random-intercept variants (RI-CLPM) are standard tools for exactly this asymmetry test. Second, natural experiments that shock an upstream node — broadband rollouts, smartphone adoption discontinuities, plant closures, austerity-driven institutional withdrawal — should move S4 with a lag; shocks that plausibly move loneliness directly (bereavement waves, pandemic isolation) should not propagate upward into D- or Y-node measures beyond what their economic content predicts. If lagged loneliness robustly predicts later declines in trust, participation, fertility intentions, or populist vote choice *after* conditioning on its own upstream causes, the downstream claim is falsified: S4 would be behaving as a driver, feeding back into the system.

Three caveats keep this from being a clean kill-shot. First, the theory's own architecture predicts feedback loops; a purist reading of "downstream symptom" that forbids any back-edge is a straw man, so the falsifiable version must specify an effect-size threshold — say, standardized cross-lagged coefficients from S4 to upstream nodes below some pre-registered fraction of the forward coefficients — rather than strict zero. Second, several upstream constructs (Y1 epistemic breakdown especially) lack instruments as mature as the loneliness scales, so asymmetry tests are handicapped by differential measurement error, which biases cross-lagged estimates in ways that can manufacture or mask feedback. Third, aggregation matters: the claim could hold at the individual level and fail at the cohort or national level, where lonely populations plausibly reshape institutions.

Conclusion: yes, falsifiable — with existing panel instruments, via pre-registered asymmetric cross-lagged tests and upstream-shock designs — provided the claim is first sharpened from "downstream, not a driver" into a quantitative asymmetry hypothesis with declared thresholds. Without that sharpening it is rhetoric; with it, the data to test it already exist, and the RI-CLPM literature on loneliness and social trust suggests the test would be genuinely at risk of failing, which is what falsifiability means.
