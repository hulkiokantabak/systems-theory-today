# RESPONSE 04

The question poses a false dichotomy, but a productive one, because working out why it is false clarifies the causal structure. The best-supported reading is a hybrid: D2 is a genuine common cause of both dynamics, yet Y1 also carries an independent, mediating pathway into Y2 that a pure common-cause model cannot absorb. The graph is D2 → Y1 → Y2 alongside D2 → Y2 directly, not a fork with a spurious Y1–Y2 edge.

Start with what the common-cause account gets right. Optimization of communication systems for engagement rather than truth (D2, amplified by D4's recommender infrastructure) degrades the epistemic commons directly: it fragments shared reference points, rewards outrage over accuracy, and selects for content that confirms rather than corrects. That is Y1. But the same optimization independently damages coordination through channels that never pass through belief at all. Engagement-sorted communication reshapes incentive structures — it makes moderate positions invisible, raises the reputational cost of compromise, and converts every negotiation into a performance before an audience selected for maximal reaction. A legislator whose beliefs are perfectly accurate still cannot coordinate if her communicative environment punishes the visible acts coordination requires. This is Y2 damage with epistemics held fixed, and it is why conditioning on Y1 would not fully screen off the D2–Y2 association.

But the common cause does not exhaust the relationship, because coordination has an epistemic precondition that is conceptually prior to incentives. Coordination requires common knowledge — not merely that parties hold accurate beliefs, but that they know what others believe and know that others know it. Epistemic breakdown attacks exactly this layer. When populations no longer share a factual baseline, actors cannot form reliable expectations about others' responses; every proposed cooperative equilibrium becomes unverifiable. Climate coordination failure is the clean case: the physical facts are stable, the incentive problems are old (they existed in acid-rain and ozone negotiations, which nonetheless succeeded), but the collapse of a shared factual substrate is new, and the coordination failure has deepened in step with it. Y1 → Y2 is doing real work here that D2 → Y2 alone cannot explain, because ozone-era communication systems were already mass media optimized for attention — what changed most between Montreal and now is the epistemic layer.

Two discriminating observations support the hybrid over either pure model. First, timing: measurable epistemic fragmentation (polarization of factual beliefs, collapse of cross-cutting media exposure) precedes the sharpest coordination breakdowns by several years in most documented cases, consistent with mediation rather than mere co-movement. Second, domain variation: coordination fails worst precisely in domains where the epistemic layer is most contested (public health, climate) and survives better where facts remain shared even under identical communication infrastructure (disaster logistics, financial plumbing). A pure common-cause model predicts uniform degradation; the mediated model predicts this gradient, and the gradient is what we see.

The synthesis matters for intervention. If Y2 were driven only by D2, repairing epistemics would be pointless — only re-engineering communication incentives would help. If Y1 alone drove Y2, epistemic repair would suffice even with engagement optimization intact. The hybrid says both levers are necessary and neither is sufficient: interventions on D2 cut off the upstream source, but accumulated epistemic damage has its own downstream momentum into coordination, and would continue to corrode Y2 even after the optimization pressure eased. Common cause and causal chain are here the same mechanism observed at two depths.
