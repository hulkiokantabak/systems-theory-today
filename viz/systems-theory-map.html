A Systems Theory
for Today

The old total systems were written by one mind, in one book. This one is built by a panel that disagrees on purpose — and by you.

The question

Since Heidegger, no single thinker has tried to explain the whole. Was that a failure of nerve — or of method?

For most of history we had total systems: theological cosmologies, then the great rational systems. A century ago the ambition fractured. The wager here is that a systems theory adequate to the 2020s can no longer be built by the old method — one mind, one book — but might be built by a new one: living, distributed, plural, computational, and continuously argued. We test it by trying to do it, using seven present-day symptoms as the pressure: fertility, dopamine, propaganda, why-now, climate, coordination, and populism.

The instrument

The panel is built to disagree

Ten thinkers, reconstructed as intellectual positions. A panel that shared a standpoint could not test the thing that matters — so this one is composed around fault lines. Pick an axis and watch the room split. The colour is the position.

      The landscape

      How the whole came apart — and where the ambition went

      The grand system did not simply vanish. It was dismantled, and then it migrated.

      –350 → 1600s

      The first total systems

      Theological cosmologies, then the encyclopedic systematizers — Aristotle mapping all of knowledge bottom-up from observation, later fused with revelation into a single ordered whole.

      1781 → 1831

      The peak: Kant to Hegel

      The last grand rational systems, built in earnest. Kant makes the order partly a contribution of the knowing mind; Hegel attempts the whole of history and spirit as one unfolding system. After Hegel (d. 1831), no one builds at that scale and is believed.

      1831 → 1979

      The five hammers

      The totality is taken apart: Nietzsche dissolves its value-ground; Gödel (1931) proves no formal system can be both complete and consistent; knowledge specializes past any one mind; Heidegger reframes the systematizing drive as the symptom; Lyotard (1979) names the mood — incredulity toward metanarratives.

      1928 → now

      The migration

      The ambition moves rather than dies — into systems science (Bertalanffy, Wiener, Prigogine, Meadows), into social-systems theory (Luhmann — the direct counter-example to "no one tried"), and into contemporary syntheses: Big History, metamodernism, and cliodynamics (Turchin), the living proof that data-driven, computational social theory is possible at all.

      What the panel seeded

      Three theories, shipped with their flaws named

      Not a consensus — three rival starting points. A is the condition, B is a mechanism inside it, C is the response. Together, they are an instance of the third one.

      Theory A

      The Adaptation Gap

      The condition · diagnosis

      The systems we build now outrun the capacity of the humans and institutions inside them. God-like tools, medieval institutions, paleolithic instincts — and the gap between them is widening. Most symptoms are downstream.

      Strength as a theory · 7/10
      Named weakness — hard to falsify. "Change is fast" can explain anything until the gap is made measurable. That's the top next task.
      Theory B

      The Optimization Ecology

      The mechanism · critique

      Markets, media, and machines have become self-reproducing optimizers that capture our drives — attention, dopamine, outrage — as fuel, and increasingly run without human ends. Selection did the designing; there is no cabal.

      Strength as a theory · 7/10
      Named weakness — conspiracy-shape. It reads like a villain story and must hold the line: selected to capture, not designed to.
      Theory C

      Distributed Coherence

      The response · reflexive

      Since no one mind holds the whole, a livable systems theory must be held as a plural, living, computational commons — not a monograph. This project is the prototype. The medium is the thesis.

      Strength as a theory · 6/10
      Named weakness — a method is not a meaning. It may route around the meaning-wound that Nietzsche insists is the ground of it all.

      The old total system is not coming back. What may be possible is this — a structured, honest, plural, self-correcting commons of sense-making, which understands the whole not by fusing it in one mind, but by holding it, argued, among many.

      Six objections kept alive, on purpose

      1. Heidegger — filling the gap with a better system only deepens the enframing.
      2. Nietzsche — every structural map routes around the meaning-wound, which is the ground the rest stands on.
      3. Turchin & Marx — a single master-problem trades testability for a satisfying story.
      4. Kant — all of it is a system of appearances; never mistake the map for the territory.
      5. Luhmann — the honest form is a theory of differentiation, not a unified doctrine; and beware a project optimizing for its own survival.
      6. Le Guin — if it becomes a machine for mastering rather than a tool for living, it has joined the disease it set out to diagnose.
